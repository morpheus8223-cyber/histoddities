## Finding 1 (the big one): subscriber threshold is already cleared

YouTube Partner Program (YPP) eligibility needs **either**:
- 1,000 subscribers **and** 4,000 valid public watch hours in the trailing 12 months, **or**
- 1,000 subscribers **and** 10 million public Shorts views in the trailing 90 days.

**The channel already has 1,360 subscribers — the subscriber bar is cleared on both paths.** The only remaining gate is watch hours or Shorts views. This reframes the entire monetization question from "grow the channel" to "accumulate one specific, controllable metric."

**Important correction to a common misconception:** Shorts watch time does **not** count toward the 4,000-hour long-form metric — they're two entirely separate qualifying paths. Growing Shorts helps subscribers, audience, and topic-validation, but it will not shrink the 4,000-hour gap. Don't let Shorts volume create a false sense of progress toward that specific number.

**Rough math on the realistic path (watch hours):** 4,000 hours = 240,000 minutes in a rolling 12-month window. A 10-minute video with realistic ~40% average retention delivers about 4 minutes of watch time per view. At that retention, **~60,000 qualifying long-form views across the trailing year** clears the bar — e.g., roughly 15–20 videos averaging 3,000–4,000 views each, sustained over the year, which is a consistency problem, not a virality problem.

**The Shorts path (10M views/90 days)** is not currently realistic from steady accumulation — the channel's best short ever got 11K views, and the entire sampled batch of 48 summed to ~176K lifetime. It would require a genuine breakout hit (which does happen in this niche — History Matters' Napoleon short did 2.3M) but shouldn't be the plan; it's a lucky bonus if it happens, not the strategy.

**Verdict: the watch-hours path via consistent long-form output is the controllable, recommended route.**

## Finding 2: a ~1-year dormancy gap is the real growth story

The 19 long-form videos cluster into three groups: five from ~2 years ago, nine from ~1 year ago, then **a roughly year-long gap with zero uploads**, then the Cadaver Synod two days ago. 1,360 subscribers after 2 years, with most of that growth in year one and none in the dormant stretch, is consistent with exactly this pattern: the channel didn't fail creatively, it went dark for a year. That gap is very likely the single biggest reason for the current numbers — bigger than any title, thumbnail, or topic choice.

**This makes cadence the highest-leverage fix available**, ahead of anything else in this document. A channel that reliably posts, even modestly, outperforms a channel with sporadic bursts of quality — both for the algorithm and for the watch-hours math above, which needs sustained output across a rolling year, not a single good month.

## Finding 3: reused third-party footage — a monetization application risk

A handful of existing Shorts use footage that isn't original to the channel: *"Step Back in Time: AI-Restored Footage of 1899 New York City in 4K"*, *"New York in the 1930's — Colorized & Restored (Credit: NASS)"* and its Part II, *"The Most Interesting Photos of the Past."* These credit a third party ("NASS") for the underlying restoration.

YouTube's Partner Program manual review explicitly screens for "reused/repetitious content" **across the whole channel**, not just per video — a handful of reused-footage uploads mixed into an otherwise-original catalog can still draw scrutiny at application time. Two low-cost options before applying:
- Confirm the licensing/permission for the "NASS" credited material is clear and say so in the description, or
- Set those specific videos to Unlisted before the YPP application, since they're a small minority and not core to the format going forward.

**Related, and worth stating directly since the channel's whole pipeline is AI-generated:** the current production method (bespoke researched scripts, custom style-locked visuals generated per topic, original narration, sourced fact-checking, deliberate per-topic style choices) sits well clear of the "AI slop" pattern YouTube's policy actually targets (single AI voice reading an unedited AI script over generic stock or templated images). Keep it that way as a standing rule, not a one-time check: vary the visual style by topic (already doing this — Cadaver Synod's oil painting vs. the Molasses Flood's Ashcan-style realism), keep the sourced-facts discipline in every script, and never mass-upload a batch of near-identical videos in a short window.

## Finding 4: the old catalog's titles actively fight discoverability

The 19 long-form titles include real typos and formatting that predate the Weird History formula work: "**Hisoddities**" and "**Histodditie**" (misspelled channel name, in the title, twice), double-pipe formatting (`||`), an emoji mid-title, and "Weird History" used as a bracketed tag on three videos — which risks read as branding confusion with the actual Weird History channel rather than an homage.

**This is a zero-cost, immediate fix:** YouTube lets you edit title/description/tags/thumbnail on a published video without losing its views, comments, or upload date. None of the 19 need to be re-uploaded. Recommend a title/thumbnail pass on the catalog using the now-established curiosity-gap formula (no channel name, no pipes, no emoji, no typos) — cheapest, fastest win available, and directly supports the watch-hours goal since it's pure upside on videos already accumulating views.

## Finding 5: the "X random facts" listicle Shorts underperform — stop making more, don't bother deleting

A large share of the ~94 existing Shorts are a template format not covered by any of the six mechanics in the Shorts analysis: "4 Weird BUT True Historical Facts!", "4 Bizarre History Facts That Sound Like Fiction!", etc. — a rotating-facts-list format. They cluster at the low-to-mid end of the channel's own range (500–10K), consistent with the earlier finding that hookless/descriptive formats underperform narrative or question formats. No need to remove them, but the production plan going forward should not add more of this format — the two series already scripted (weird-laws, question one-shots) are stronger uses of the same 40-second budget.
