**NARRATOR:** In January of the year 897, a court convened in Rome to try a man for perjury. There was a judge. There were lawyers. There was a defendant, dressed in his finest robes, seated in the courtroom.

There was just one tiny procedural issue.

The defendant had been dead for seven months.

**[VISUAL: Record-scratch freeze frame on the corpse. Text overlay: "yes, really."]**

And no, this wasn't some symbolic trial where they read charges to an empty chair. The Pope had the actual body of the previous Pope dug up, dressed in full papal vestments, propped up on a throne — and cross-examined.

This is the story of the Cadaver Synod: the moment the papacy hit rock bottom, and then kept digging. Literally.

## CHAPTER 1: HOW DO YOU END UP TRYING A CORPSE? (0:50–3:00)

**[ON-SCREEN CHAPTER CARD: "Rome, 897 AD: Not Great"]**

**NARRATOR:** To understand how the Catholic Church ended up litigating against a dead body, you need to understand that the ninth century was not exactly the papacy's golden era.

**[VISUAL: Map of Italy fragmenting into competing duchies; arrows for Spoleto, Frankish factions.]**

After Charlemagne's empire fell apart, Italy became a knife fight between noble families — and the papacy was the prize. Being pope meant land, money, and armies. So the job stopped going to the holiest man in Rome and started going to whoever's family could muscle him onto the throne. Between 872 and 965, there were more than two dozen popes. For reference, that's roughly the turnover rate of a fast food franchise, except the exit interviews involved considerably more strangulation.

**[VISUAL: Portrait/engraving of Pope Formosus.]**

Enter Formosus. And yes, his name literally means "good-looking" in Latin, which historians assure us is the last flattering thing about this era. Formosus was, by the standards of the time, actually competent — a skilled diplomat and missionary who was so popular that the people of Bulgaria specifically requested him as their archbishop.

That popularity was the problem. A previous pope, John VIII, saw Formosus as a rival, accused him of — among other things — "conspiring to seek the papacy," and excommunicated him. Formosus fled Rome, got un-excommunicated, worked his way back, and in 891... became pope anyway.

**[VISUAL: Text overlay: "conspiring to seek the papacy" → checkmark → "well."]**

**NARRATOR:** Now here's the technicality that matters, so hang onto it: church law at the time said a bishop couldn't transfer from one seat to another. Formosus was already bishop of Porto when he took the papacy. Which meant that, on paper, one could argue his entire papacy was illegal.

Nobody made that argument while he was alive, of course. It's the kind of argument you make about someone once they can no longer respond. Which — spoiler — is exactly where this is going.

## CHAPTER 2: THE WRONG PEOPLE GET CROWNED (3:00–4:40)

**[ON-SCREEN CHAPTER CARD: "A Coronation He'd Regret"]**

**NARRATOR:** As pope, Formosus had one job that kept blowing up in his face: deciding who to crown Holy Roman Emperor. The Duke of Spoleto — a man named Guy, because history is occasionally hilarious — strong-armed Formosus into crowning first him, and then his teenage son Lambert, as co-emperors.

**[VISUAL: Cutout animation — Formosus reluctantly placing a crown while a large armored Guy looms behind him.]**

Formosus did not enjoy being the Spoleto family's personal crown dispenser. So he secretly invited a rival — Arnulf, King of the East Franks — to march on Italy and take the crown instead. Arnulf did, Formosus crowned HIM emperor in 896, and for about five minutes, the plan worked.

Then Arnulf suffered a paralyzing stroke and had to go home. And in April 896, Formosus died — officially of natural causes, though given the era, "natural causes" carried an asterisk roughly the size of the Colosseum.

Which left one very powerful, very furious family — the Spoletos — holding a grudge against a man who was now conveniently beyond the reach of consequences.

Or so he thought.

## CHAPTER 3: THE TRIAL OF THE MILLENNIUM (4:40–7:20)

**[ON-SCREEN CHAPTER CARD: "The Cadaver Synod"]**

**NARRATOR:** After a brief interlude — including one pope, Boniface VI, who lasted fifteen whole days — the throne went to Stephen VI, a man backed by, you guessed it, the Spoleto family.

And in January 897, Stephen ordered the thing that gets this video made eleven hundred years later: dig him up.

**[VISUAL: Laurens painting again, now with slow pans across each figure as they're described.]**

Formosus' corpse — seven months into decomposition — was exhumed, dressed head to toe in full papal vestments, carried into the Basilica of St. John Lateran, and propped upright on a throne to stand trial. Church officials were required to attend. A teenage deacon was appointed to stand behind the body and answer questions on its behalf, a job description we can only assume was left out of his seminary brochure.

**[VISUAL: Cutout animation of the deacon peeking out from behind the throne.]**

Stephen served as both prosecutor and judge — a courtroom arrangement lawyers describe as "not ideal." By most accounts he screamed the charges directly at the corpse: perjury, coveting the papacy, and — here's that technicality — illegally transferring from the bishopric of Porto to the papal throne.

The corpse, in what legal historians consider a bold strategy, said nothing.

Formosus was found guilty on all counts. And the sentence is where this goes from dark comedy to just dark. His papacy was declared null and void — every ordination he'd performed, invalidated, which threw the careers of clergy all over Europe into chaos. The three fingers of his right hand — the fingers he used to give blessings — were cut off. He was stripped of the vestments, dressed in commoner's rags, and buried in a graveyard for foreigners.

Then Stephen changed his mind, had him dug up AGAIN, and thrown into the Tiber River.

**[VISUAL: Map graphic — Lateran → graveyard → river, with an increasingly exasperated dotted line.]**

**NARRATOR:** That's right. Formosus was buried more times after his death than most popes are elected.

## CHAPTER 4: INSTANT KARMA (7:20–9:00)

**[ON-SCREEN CHAPTER CARD: "It Backfires Immediately"]**

**NARRATOR:** If Stephen expected Rome to applaud, he had badly misread the room. Even by ninth-century standards — a low bar, resting comfortably on the floor — putting a corpse on trial struck people as, and we quote the general mood here, "too much."

Then the omens started. Legend says a hermit fished Formosus' body out of the Tiber and gave it a quiet burial, and that the corpse began performing miracles. And around the same time, the Basilica of St. John Lateran — the very courtroom where the synod was held — was severely damaged by an earthquake.

**[VISUAL: Engraving of the collapsed basilica. Text overlay: "subtle."]**

Rome took the hint even if Stephen didn't. Within months, a mob rose up, stripped Stephen of his papal insignia, and threw him in prison — where, in the summer of 897, he was strangled. The man who spent his papacy prosecuting a corpse ended his papacy by becoming one.

**[VISUAL: Beat of silence. Cutout of the scales of justice, wobbling, then leveling.]**

**NARRATOR:** His successors spent years cleaning up the mess. Pope Theodore II — reign length: twenty days, and this still made the highlight reel — annulled the synod and had Formosus reburied in St. Peter's with full honors. Pope John IX went further, annulling it again and banning trials of dead people, a law you'd be forgiven for assuming already existed.

And then, because the ninth century simply would not let anyone have nice things, Pope Sergius III — who had participated in the original synod as a bishop — took power in 904 and reaffirmed the conviction. Some accounts even claim he wrote a flattering epitaph for Stephen's tomb. History's pettiest sequel.

## OUTRO (9:00–10:00)

**[VISUAL: Slow pull-back on the Laurens painting, matching the cold open.]**

**NARRATOR:** The Cadaver Synod kicked off what historians politely call the papacy's "nadir" — several decades of coups, murders, and mysteriously brief pontificates. It took over a century for the office to fully recover its dignity, and Formosus himself was never formally re-convicted or fully cleared. To this day, his case remains technically... complicated. Which is impressive, for a defendant who's been dead for more than eleven hundred years.

- **Chapter cards on screen** at each section break — they double as retention anchors and make the video easy to recut into Shorts (the cold open + Chapter 3 both work standalone as Shorts).
- **Fact-check flags:** Stephen is numbered VI or VII depending on source (VI is standard). "Corpse performed miracles" and the Sergius III epitaph are *legends* reported in later chronicles — the script hedges them with "legend says" / "some accounts claim," keep that hedging in the VO.
