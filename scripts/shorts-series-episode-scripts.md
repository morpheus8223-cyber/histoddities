# SERIES A — "Laws That Exist Because Someone Did The Thing"
(Pilot = Cadaver Synod short 3. Episode titles keep the repeatable format. Pinned comment on every episode: "What's the weirdest 'why does this law exist' law you know?")

## A1 — It's Illegal To Wear Armor In Parliament
1. "England has a law that bans wearing a full suit of armor into Parliament. It has been in force for over seven hundred years."
   — Still: a knight in full plate armor standing awkwardly at the door of a medieval parliament chamber, seated robed lords staring. Motion: slow push-in, candle flicker.
2. "The year was thirteen thirteen. King Edward the Second had a problem: his barons kept attending Parliament dressed for war."
   — Still: rows of armored barons seated in a great hall, hands on sword hilts, tense king on throne. Motion: slow pan across the armored rows.
3. "Debates about taxes hit differently when the opposition benches are wearing swords. So Edward banned bringing armor and weapons entirely."
   — Still: a royal clerk sealing a decree while soldiers stack swords and helmets in a pile by the door. Motion: candle gutter, slow zoom on the pile.
4. "The statute was never repealed. Seven centuries later, it is still technically illegal. Parliament has apparently decided not to risk it."
   — Still: the empty modern-medieval chamber at night, one suit of armor standing in shadow by the entrance like a warning. Motion: very slow pull-back.
   Fact base: Statute Forbidding Bearing of Armour, 1313 (7 Edw. II), still nominally in force.

## A2 — Every Whale In Britain Legally Belongs To The King
1. "If a whale washes up on a British beach, it does not belong to the finder. By law, it belongs to the King."
   — Still: a beached whale on a moonlit shore, tiny figures with lanterns gathered around it. Motion: waves lap, lantern flicker.
2. "The law dates to thirteen twenty-four. Whales and sturgeons were declared royal fish, property of the Crown, wherever they landed."
   — Still: a medieval king's officials in fine robes claiming an enormous fish from grumbling fishermen at a dock. Motion: slow pan along the fish.
3. "Why? Because whale carcasses were floating gold: oil, bone, and meat. And fishermen kept quietly keeping them."
   — Still: fishermen by torchlight carving a whale on a beach, one nervously looking over his shoulder. Motion: torch flames stream in wind.
4. "The law is still in force. In two thousand four, a Welsh fisherman who caught a sturgeon was investigated. For stealing from the Queen."
   — Still: a lone fisherman holding a large sturgeon, a shadow of a crown looming on the wall behind him. Motion: slow push-in on his worried face.
   Fact base: Prerogativa Regis c. 1324, "royal fish"; 2004 Robert Davies sturgeon case (investigated, no charges).

## A3 — America Once Forced Margarine To Be Dyed Pink
1. "In the eighteen eighties, several American states passed a law requiring margarine to be dyed bright pink. This was not a joke."
   — Still: a shocked Victorian grocer holding a plate of lurid pink butter-like substance, customers recoiling. Motion: slow zoom on the pink block.
2. "Margarine was new, cheap, and butter-colored. Dairy farmers were furious. Sellers kept passing it off as real butter."
   — Still: a sneaky Victorian merchant sliding a golden block across a counter while a farmer glares through the window. Motion: candle flicker, shadow creep.
3. "So the dairy lobby struck back: if margarine could not look like butter, make it look ridiculous. Pink. Undeniably, unappetizingly pink."
   — Still: stern legislators in a smoky chamber holding up a pink block like evidence at trial. Motion: smoke drifts, slow push-in.
4. "The Supreme Court struck the pink laws down in eighteen ninety-eight. Margarine survived. The dairy lobby, historians note, never forgave it."
   — Still: a triumphant pale golden margarine block on a pedestal in dramatic light, pink paint dripping off it. Motion: slow dramatic push-in.
   Fact base: state anti-margarine laws 1880s (Vermont, NH, WV pink-dye laws); Collins v. New Hampshire (1898) struck down; federal Margarine Act 1886 taxed it.

## A4 — Rome Banned Crying Too Hard At Funerals
1. "Ancient Rome passed a law banning women from wailing too loudly at funerals. Because the mourners were being paid to do it."
   — Still: dramatic Roman funeral procession, veiled women mid-wail with theatrical anguish, torches, a bemused magistrate watching. Motion: torch flicker, slow pan.
2. "Rich Romans hired professional mourners to make funerals look important. The louder the grief, the greater the man. Obviously."
   — Still: a wealthy Roman handing coins to a line of women rehearsing sorrowful poses beside a tomb. Motion: coin glint, candle sway.
3. "Funerals became arms races: torn cheeks, shredded robes, screaming. So the Twelve Tables, Rome's first law code, banned the theatrics."
   — Still: enormous bronze tablets of law looming over a subdued, orderly funeral procession. Motion: slow tilt down the tablets.
4. "That was nearly twenty-five centuries ago. It remains history's only law against being too sad. Professionally sad, anyway."
   — Still: a single dignified mourner with one restrained tear beside a modest Roman grave, moonlight. Motion: very slow push-in.
   Fact base: Twelve Tables (c. 450 BC), Table X funeral restrictions (banned laceration of cheeks, excessive lamentation; per Cicero, De Legibus II).

## A5 — Henry VIII Made Tennis Illegal (For You)
1. "In fifteen forty-one, England made tennis illegal. Unless you were rich. King Henry the Eighth, a keen tennis player, signed it personally."
   — Still: a lavish king mid-swing on a royal tennis court while guards turn away shabby commoners at the gate. Motion: slow pan from king to gate.
2. "The problem was national security. England's army ran on longbows, and the law required commoners to practice archery every week."
   — Still: rows of English yeomen at archery butts at dusk, arrows in flight, a sergeant counting heads. Motion: arrows streak, banners sway.
3. "But archery practice was losing badly to tennis, bowls, and skittles. So Parliament simply banned the competition. All of it."
   — Still: sheriffs confiscating racquets, bowls and dice from a guilty-looking tavern crowd. Motion: candle flicker, slow zoom on the pile of confiscated games.
4. "The Unlawful Games Act stayed on the books for three hundred years. The king, of course, kept his court. And his racquet."
   — Still: the king's private tennis court glowing at night, one racquet resting on a velvet cushion like a crown jewel. Motion: slow push-in.
   Fact base: Unlawful Games Act 1541 (33 Hen. VIII c. 9), protecting archery practice; largely repealed 1845; Henry VIII's Hampton Court tennis court.

# SERIES B — Question One-Shots ("Why did...?")
(Each doubles as longform topic validation — a 48h winner is the next longform candidate. Pinned comment per episode below.)

## B1 — Why Did Napoleon Retreat From Rabbits?
1. "Napoleon Bonaparte conquered most of Europe. In eighteen oh seven, he was defeated in open field. By rabbits."
   — Still: Napoleon in iconic bicorne hat recoiling as a tide of rabbits swarms toward him across a field. Motion: slow push-in on his alarm.
2. "His chief of staff organized a celebration hunt and, the story goes, bought thousands of rabbits. Farm rabbits. This detail matters."
   — Still: soldiers unloading crates upon crates of rabbits from wagons onto a manicured lawn. Motion: crate lids tremble.
3. "Wild rabbits flee humans. Farm rabbits associate humans with food. The cages opened, and the entire horde charged the Emperor."
   — Still: a furry wave of rabbits flowing like cavalry toward the imperial party, officers scattering. Motion: the wave surges slowly forward.
4. "Witnesses say they flanked him in two columns. The conqueror of Europe retreated to his carriage. The rabbits held the field."
   — Still: Napoleon's carriage racing away at dusk, triumphant rabbits dotting the field behind it like a victorious army. Motion: slow pull-back wide.
   Pinned: "Which do you want next: the full rabbit incident, or the war Australia lost to actual birds?"
   Fact base: July 1807 hunt arranged by Berthier after Tilsit; account from period memoirs — script hedges with "the story goes."

## B2 — Why Did A Wave Of Beer Destroy A London Neighborhood?
1. "In eighteen fourteen, a fifteen-foot wave tore through a London neighborhood, smashing houses. The wave was made of beer."
   — Still: a towering dark foaming wave bursting through a brick brewery wall into a narrow street, figures fleeing. Motion: foam churns slowly.
2. "A brewery vat twenty-two feet tall ruptured, taking neighboring vats with it. Over three hundred thousand gallons of porter, released at once."
   — Still: colossal wooden vats inside a candlelit brewery, one iron hoop snapping, staves bursting. Motion: slow zoom, dust and mist.
3. "The flood struck the crowded slum of St Giles. It flattened two houses and killed eight people. London smelled of beer for months."
   — Still: a devastated narrow street, beer flowing between damaged houses, residents wading knee-deep by lantern light. Motion: liquid glimmer, lantern flicker.
4. "The court ruled it an act of God. The brewery paid nothing — and got its beer tax refunded. History's darkest happy hour."
   — Still: barristers in a candlelit courtroom shrugging before a judge, a beer barrel sitting in the dock like a defendant. Motion: slow push-in on the barrel.
   Pinned: "Worse way to go: the beer flood, or the molasses wave that hit Boston a century later? (That one's coming.)"
   Fact base: London Beer Flood, Oct 17 1814, Meux's Horse Shoe Brewery; ~320,000+ gallons; 8 dead; "Act of God" ruling; excise rebate granted.

## B3 — Why Did Molasses Outrun People Through Boston?
1. "In nineteen nineteen, a wave of molasses moved through Boston at thirty-five miles per hour. People could not outrun it."
   — Still: a massive dark glossy wave rolling down an early-1900s city street, figures sprinting before it, elevated railway behind. Motion: the wave advances slowly.
2. "A steel tank holding two point three million gallons split open. Witnesses said the rivets popped like machine-gun fire."
   — Still: an enormous riveted industrial tank bursting at the seams, dark liquid exploding outward, rivets flying. Motion: slow zoom on the rupture.
3. "The wave was twenty-five feet tall. It crushed buildings, tipped a railcar, and killed twenty-one people. In molasses."
   — Still: aftermath street scene half-drowned in gleaming brown flood, rescuers wading, a buckled elevated track. Motion: liquid sheen shifts, slow pan.
4. "The disaster forced America to invent modern engineering inspections. Every certified building you enter is, in a sense, molasses-proof."
   — Still: sober engineers with blueprints inspecting a new tank, the ruined old tank's skeleton behind them at dusk. Motion: blueprint flutter, slow pull-back.
   Pinned: "Full longform on the Great Molasses Flood: yes or no? The comments decide."
   Fact base: Great Molasses Flood, Jan 15 1919, North End Boston; 2.3M gallons; ~35 mph; 21 dead, 150 injured; led to engineering certification laws.

## B4 — Why Did The Shortest War In History Last 38 Minutes?
1. "The shortest war in recorded history began at two minutes past nine in the morning. By nine forty, it was over."
   — Still: British warships in a tropical harbor at golden morning light, smoke beginning to rise from a palace on shore. Motion: smoke drifts, water glitters.
2. "Eighteen ninety-six, Zanzibar. The sultan died, his cousin Khalid seized the palace, and Britain, who had not approved him, sent an ultimatum."
   — Still: a defiant robed figure at a palace window, harem guards barricading doors with furniture, ticking clock on the wall. Motion: curtain sway, slow push-in.
3. "Khalid barricaded himself in with three thousand men and one royal yacht. Britain had five warships. The math was unkind."
   — Still: the lone ornate royal yacht dwarfed by a line of grey warships across the harbor. Motion: slow pan down the line of ships.
4. "The bombardment lasted thirty-eight minutes. The palace fell, the yacht sank, and Khalid fled to the German consulate before breakfast was cold."
   — Still: the ruined smoking palace at mid-morning, a small figure sprinting down a side street clutching his robes. Motion: smoke rises, slow pull-back.
   Pinned: "38 minutes. The full story (including what happened to Khalid) could be a longform — want it?"
   Fact base: Anglo-Zanzibar War, Aug 27 1896; ~09:02–09:40; Sultan Khalid bin Barghash; ~500 defender casualties; fled to German consulate.

## B5 — Why Did It Rain Meat In Kentucky?
1. "On March third, eighteen seventy-six, chunks of red meat fell from a clear Kentucky sky for several minutes. This is documented."
   — Still: a farm woman in an apron staring upward as dark flakes drift down over a farmhouse yard, clear sky. Motion: flakes fall slowly.
2. "The flakes were up to four inches wide. Two local men tasted it. They pronounced it venison, or possibly mutton. They tasted it."
   — Still: two weathered farmers examining a scrap of meat in one's palm with grave scholarly expressions. Motion: slow push-in on the palm.
3. "Scientists floated theories: cosmic matter, dried frog spawn. The real answer was worse. Look up. What flies and carries meat?"
   — Still: dark birds circling high in the sky above the farm, ominous silhouettes against pale clouds. Motion: birds wheel slowly.
4. "Vultures. A flock had disgorged mid-flight, and vultures disgorge in chain reactions. It did not rain meat. It rained vulture lunch."
   — Still: a row of vultures perched on a fence at sunset looking guilty, scattered scraps on the grass below. Motion: feather ruffle, very slow zoom.
   Pinned: "Two men TASTED it. Drop your theory for what possesses a person. Full weird-weather longform if this hits 100K."
   Fact base: Kentucky meat shower, Mar 3 1876, Olympia Springs, Bath County; reported in Scientific American/NYT; leading explanation vulture disgorgement.
